Independent Study & Mentorship

ISM is a research-based course where students choose a career field, study it in depth, and work with a professional mentor in that field. Instead of following a set curriculum, each student designs their own learning: reading industry sources, interviewing professionals, documenting what they learn, and creating an original product that shows real understanding.

My field is private equity. I joined ISM in August 2026, and this portfolio documents my progress through the year.

  1. Stage 1Research

    Learn the field through primary and secondary sources.

  2. Stage 2Interviews

    Talk with finance professionals about their work.

  3. Stage 3Mentorship

    Work alongside a mentor in the industry.

  4. Stage 4Final product

    Create and present original work.

Research assessments

Assessment #1 · Current State of the Finance IndustryAug 28, 2026

What is happening right now? The finance industry is quickly adopting smart automation and real-time data management to help customers manage their finances. Banks are moving beyond rule-based chatbots to autonomous financial systems called “agentic AI,” which can handle routine financial operations without constant human supervision. This year, financial experts are discussing how to use these systems to build investment portfolios, assess market risk, and give customers personalized recommendations. Classic finance careers are still in demand: according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, jobs for financial analysts will grow 7 percent over the next decade.

What’s disrupting the field? New technology is causing considerable disruption. Software coding agents and development tools let banks build their own software to speed up transaction processing and client service (McKinsey & Company). This fast transition also brings serious cyber threats and fraud. As daily financial processes become automated, protecting clients’ personal information from synthetic identity theft has become urgent, and risk management is now one of the most important issues in corporate banking. Executives are constantly balancing innovation with government regulation.

Old ways vs. new ways. Analysts used to spend hours manually collecting data, entering it into spreadsheets, and writing reports by hand. Finding patterns took days, and reports came out every few months. Today, software does difficult calculations in seconds, and analysts have dynamic systems with market figures available immediately. Instead of calculating, finance specialists now spend their time analyzing automatically generated data and explaining it to clients.

Looking ahead. Finance will depend more and more on automation for daily banking, trading, and investing. That makes human abilities like critical thinking, communication, and professional ethics even more important. This research changed my view of the field: working in finance is less about doing arithmetic and more about using technology responsibly in the corporate and finance world.

McKinsey & Company. (2025, March 12). The state of AI: How organizations are rewiring to capture value.

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Financial analysts: Occupational outlook handbook.

Assessment #2 · Annotated Bibliography: PE Operational Impacts & Market DynamicsSep 11, 2026

Davis, Steven J., et al. “Private Equity, Jobs, and Productivity.” American Economic Review, vol. 104, no. 12, Dec. 2014, pp. 3956–90.

Before reading this study, I believed leveraged buyouts only led to large-scale layoffs with no operational benefits. The Census-level data from Davis et al. changed my mind. Buyouts play a vital role in creating and reallocating jobs, which shows that the real advantage of private equity is improving total factor productivity, not just cutting staff. The dataset ends in 2005, so it is less relevant to today’s costly capital market. Next, I plan to study operational models after 2020.

Kannan, Sneha, et al. “Changes in Hospital Adverse Events and Patient Outcomes Associated with Private Equity Acquisition.” JAMA, vol. 330, no. 24, 26 Dec. 2023, pp. 2365–75.

This paper showed me the human cost when operational efficiency becomes the priority. I was surprised by a 27% increase in patient falls and a 38% increase in central line infections after private equity acquisitions. I had thought financial restructuring only changed administrative processes, not service quality. Bloodstream infections rose even as procedures declined, which points to an operational cause. This shows the limits of PE in human-focused sectors. My next step is to contact a healthcare administrator about how staffing changes after an acquisition.

“What Is Middle Market Private Equity? Deal Size & Strategies.” Carta, 3 Apr. 2026.

This source gave me the key terminology and mechanics of middle-market private equity, including the “buy-and-build” strategy, add-on acquisitions, and multiple arbitrage. However, Carta takes a fairly positive view of direct lending and leverage and leaves out the risks of over-leveraging a company. I learned to watch for bias in sources written by service providers.

“U.S. Private Equity at Mid-Year 2026: Activity Held, Value Collapsed.” Cherry Bekaert, 17 Aug. 2026.

This report shifted my view of current deal structures. I assumed PE firms were constantly working on large take-private deals, but the 90.1% drop in large-cap deals showed me they are being cautious in this economy. Add-ons now make up about three-quarters of buyout activity, so firms are moving toward smaller, lower-risk platforms. I plan to research whether this strategy will last.

“Unlocking Retail Access to Private Equity Investments through Defined Contribution Plans.” Economic Report of the President, Council of Economic Advisers, Apr. 2026, ch. 12.

This report looks at private equity in retail 401(k) plans. It makes a strong case using the Public Market Equivalent measure, but I still question whether these investments are safe for the average retirement saver. With 7 to 10 year lock-ups and high costs, the lack of liquidity seems to contradict the purpose of a 401(k). Reading about Executive Order 14330 and Anderson v. Intel Corp. showed me how fast the rules are changing while their legality is still being settled.

Assessment #3 · Middle Market Private EquitySep 18, 2026

The Carta article “What Is Middle Market Private Equity? Deal Size & Strategies” explains how private equity funds create value by acquiring mid-sized companies and then growing and professionalizing them. Unlike early-stage venture capital, which backs young startups with unproven models and high failure risk, and unlike large-cap private equity, which relies on financial engineering, take-private deals, and heavy leverage, middle market private equity focuses on operational transformation. Its targets are already established and profitable, with stable, predictable cash flows, but they often lack professional executives, software, reporting, and financial management systems. Middle market firms act as strategic partners and help management directly.

This content connects closely to corporate finance theory and to my ISM topic: private equity, valuation, cash flow management, and portfolio management. Middle market firms create equity value through operational levers rather than relying on economic growth or aggressive leverage. The article highlights the “buy and build” strategy, where a firm buys a platform company and makes smaller add-on acquisitions to build market presence, consolidate the industry, improve the supply chain, and raise enterprise value.

Strong, reliable cash flow is the foundation of these investments. It lets portfolio companies pay interest on their debt while keeping enough liquidity to reinvest, funding growth such as hiring senior executives like CFOs, building ERP systems, improving procurement, and expanding into new markets.

The article also covers the back-office and fund administration work behind private equity. Fund administrators must track capital calls and cap tables across complex bolt-ons, comply with valuation standards like ASC 820, and turn financial data from different acquired companies into standardized metrics for LPs. Overall, middle market private equity is an active asset class where returns depend on scalable infrastructure, efficient cash flow management, good governance, and successfully transforming mid-sized companies into efficient institutions.

Assessment #4 · Annotated Bibliography: PE Operational Impacts & Market DynamicsSep 25, 2026

Bhatla, Anjali, et al. “Changes in Patient Care Experience After Private Equity Acquisition of US Hospitals.” JAMA, vol. 333, no. 5, 2025, pp. 490–498.

This article showed me how operational change affects patient experience beyond clinical measures. I assumed cost-cutting would mainly show up in infection rates, but Bhatla et al. found that patient experience ratings consistently drop after an acquisition, especially for staff responsiveness. Cost-saving changes affect care in ways clinical measures miss.

Gupta, Atul, et al. “Does Private Equity Investment in Healthcare Improve Patient Outcomes? Evidence from Nursing Homes.” The Review of Financial Studies, vol. 34, no. 9, Sept. 2021, pp. 4382–4441.

This paper made me rethink the effects of pushing efficiency too far in care facilities. I believed PE acquisitions focused on management and property savings without affecting mortality, but Gupta et al. found that PE acquisitions of nursing homes led to higher patient mortality and fewer staff hours. Next, I’ll look at how state staffing mandates affect PE investment in assisted living.

“Private Equity Report: 2025 Trends and 2026 Outlook.” Cherry Bekaert, 25 Feb. 2026.

This outlook changed my view of how macroeconomics shapes PE strategy after 2024. I thought high interest rates would always reduce deal flow, but managers are adapting by deploying dry powder into platform consolidation. My next task is to compare carve-out transactions with take-private transactions.

“Opening the 401(k) Gates: The Potential Promise and Perils of Alternative Investments.” Freshfields Bruckhaus Deringer, 14 Aug. 2025.

This analysis showed me that bringing private market investments into retail retirement accounts is harder than I thought, and illiquidity is not the only obstacle. Changing Department of Labor rules and ERISA litigation risk create difficult fiduciary issues for plan sponsors trying to balance high returns with investor protection.

Erel, Isil, et al. “How Does Private Equity Affect Firm Value and Performance?” Annual Review of Financial Economics, vol. 13, no. 1, Nov. 2021, pp. 237–260.

This review gave me a balanced way to assess private equity’s net effect on company efficiency. I thought efficiency gains came only from financial leverage, but Erel et al. show that operational changes and acquisitions contribute equally to revenue improvements. I’ll use this source when weighing efficiency against service quality.

Reading the Room: Relative Valuation and Comparable Company Analysis

Last week I constructed a DCF model for estimating the intrinsic value of the company. For this week, however, I studied another side of valuation - relative valuation. DCF model tries to determine how much is the company worth based on its own future cash flows, while relative valuation tries to figure out how much is the market willing to pay for other companies which are similar. I concentrated on comps and multiples used there (EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/Revenue).

The most important lesson that I learned was why the EV/EBITDA ratio is better for private equity firms than the P/E ratio. The reason behind this is that the enterprise value incorporates debt and EBITDA is earned prior to interest expense. Thus, EV/EBITDA ratio allows comparing companies with varying capital structures when considering a leveraged buyout.

This made me wonder a few things. How is it that analysts pick “comparable” firms to use for valuations, especially since no two firms are comparable because of differences in their scale, growth, and margins? To what extent do entry and exit multiples impact the returns from a PE investment?

The major problem was that I needed to find an appropriate peer group. My initial comp group included companies with high growth rates along with stable companies, which resulted in high variation in multiples. In order to resolve this issue, I selected my peer group based on industries, revenues, and growth rates. Moreover, I took median rather than average multiple.

My most important achievement was valuing a business using comps and my previous DCF, followed by an explanation of the reason behind their discrepancy. Moving forward, I will learn about precedent transactions and the fundamentals of a leveraged buyout.

September 28, 2026 · Post 03

Revelation of Intrinsic Value: Application of the DCF Methodology

As part of my previous research on the balance sheets and operating cash flow statements, this week I learned about one more important method of valuation, the DCF methodology. Contrary to the other methods previously analyzed, the DCF model determines the intrinsic value of a company based on its projected Free Cash Flows.

The core of my research was the process of discounting future cash flows to their present value based on the WACC. This helped me understand the impact of any changes in macroeconomic conditions, especially interest rate changes, on a company's discount rate.

The difficulty I faced while developing the model was in setting realistic assumptions concerning the growth and revenue projections, which were critical due to their significant impact on the results. The solution to the problem was finding industry benchmarks and doing the sensitivity analysis.

The success of my work was in developing a functional DCF model based on real data from 10-Ks. As my next step, I plan to analyze some relative valuation measures, such as EV/EBITDA and P/E ratios.

September 7, 2026 · Post 02

Driving Cash Flow – The Engine of Valuation

Extending from last week's Balance Sheet analysis, I explored this week the topic of the Cash Flow Statement. Whereas balance sheets are static in nature, operating cash flows provide insight into the cash engine that powers operations. Specifically, I looked into the concepts of Operating Cash Flow, Free Cash Flow (FCF), and the Cash Conversion Cycle.

The issues arose in terms of practical use, such as what is the basis of comparison for FCF between capital-intensive manufacturers and software companies? Another interest of mine is the impact of working capital fluctuations on stability in economic changes.

The biggest difficulty in this topic came from learning the indirect approach for calculating net income adjusted to Operating Cash Flow. Non-cash items were confusing at first, which I have overcome through a detailed step-by-step adjustment of net income from historical 10-K reports.

The biggest achievement was in calculating the Free Cash Flows of three different companies independently from any tools or software. This coming week, I will learn the basics of cash flows in order to model Discounted Cash Flow valuation.

August 24, 2026 · Post 01

Decoding the Balance Sheet: Evaluating Corporate Financial Health

During the past week, the topic of my independent study on finance was the basics of equity analysis, where I learned how to analyze the financial health of a publicly traded firm using the major balance sheet metrics. Rather than analyzing the current market situation, I examined how ratios such as the Current Ratio, Quick Ratio, and Debt-to-Equity ratio can show a company's underlying liquidity and leverage. The ability to distinguish between short-term debts and long-term liabilities made it easier to understand how institutional investors determine the creditworthiness of a business before investing in it.

This week's investigation led to several important questions concerning practical application. Namely, how do the financial analysts adapt liquidity estimates of companies in different industries with various levels of inventory turnover and assets? Also, I am interested in how shifts in the macro-economy, such as changes in the interest rate, influence the optimal capital structure of the company.

The main challenge I encountered during this week was related to the interpretation of the notes included in the corporate filings of the company with the SEC, where there can be information on off-balance-sheet liabilities and complex lease agreements. To solve this problem, I familiarized myself with the SEC reporting requirements and the financial analysis manuals.

The main success of this week is associated with the ability to calculate the financial ratios and interpret their values independently without the assistance of specialized software. Next week, I need to learn to scan 10-K reports faster and analyze the cash flow statements more thoroughly. In the future, my goal is to learn more about the DCF model for intrinsic valuation.
